rebuilding visibility

A Nordic software company building enterprise asset management tools.

overview

I worked as a UX/UI designer for four months on a software product used by teams managing aquaculture operations across multiple sites in Denmark. The software assigns tasks to field personnel and gives managers visibility into task status, deviations, and risk across locations.

the problem
3

separate software systems, different vendors, none sharing data.

12

data-type views on one dashboard, with location only available as a filter on top.

18

separate widgets on a single asset's dashboard, most of them empty.

The old systems organised by data type, not place. Location was a filter, not a structure. A manager checking on one specific site still had to dig through unrelated categories one at a time, even after choosing a location.

Each category led to its own view, designed for a manager who already knew what kind of data he was looking for - but most managers just wanted to know what was happening at a place.

the shift

Location used to sit behind a filter. The goal was to make it the first thing a manager saw.

the redesign

The redesign starts with where, not what kind of data. Picking a location surfaces what matters about that place immediately, instead of narrowing down a list of unrelated categories.

A manager lands on a map of every location, or switches to a list view with the same data, shaped differently. Only one view shows at a time, toggled with this control.

Simplified structure - location first, all detail nested underneath.

At the asset level, a manager used to scroll past a wall of counters, most of them at zero, looking for the one that wasn't. Now they see a health score and a risk plot first, so the assets that need attention stand out without anyone having to scan for them. Tasks list their location right there, instead of somewhere else. And if something actually goes wrong at a site, the note about it sits on the same page as the data, not buried in a separate messages tab.

the resistance
01
Before testing, the client-side point of contact resisted the new structure. The concern wasn't the design itself - it was adoption risk. Long-time managers were used to the old system, and a transition risked slowing down task execution exactly when it mattered most.
02
The new structure was tested with the field managers who'd actually use it daily. They pushed back too - not because anything was broken, but because the structure was unfamiliar. They wanted certainty they were doing things correctly, and a new look threatened that.
03
What actually moved both groups was time, not a better argument. A multi-day, section-by-section walkthrough ran over video calls, deliberately paced so people could sit with the change rather than approve it on the spot - paired with concrete proof. Managers reached key actions in fewer steps. Risk appeared immediately instead of staying buried.
what i'd measure

No instrumentation existed to track this quantitatively during the project. If it had, two numbers worth watching:

Task completion rate
On the assignment flow - does a field operative reach confirmation without backtracking or dropping out?
Time-to-locate
How many clicks a manager needs to reach the current status of a specific site from a cold start. Under the old structure, this required choosing a category before choosing a place.
